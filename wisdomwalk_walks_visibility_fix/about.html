The Intention

How We Walk

How we walk shapes how we see, and how we meet the world.

We design conditions — the kind of quiet, the kind of slowness, the kind of attention — in which something real can happen. What that something is, we cannot predict. And we have learned not to try.

Every walk we offer is shaped by a set of principles: not just in what we do, but in how we are while doing it. These are not rules. They are orientations — ways of being in relationship with the land, with ourselves, and with each other.

I

We move slowly

Speed is a kind of forgetting. When we move fast, we miss the texture of a stone underfoot, the way a valley holds sound differently at midday than at dusk, the point at which the body begins to settle into its own rhythm. Our walks are not designed to cover ground. They are designed to uncover it — slowly, on foot, in the kind of silence that opens naturally when there is nowhere else to be.

II

We listen before we lead

We come to each land as students, not experts. The mountains of Peru do not need our interpretation. The olive groves of the Aegean do not need our narrative. What they need, and what we practice, is the willingness to be taught. Our guides know the land deeply. But their first offering is always silence — the kind that makes room for the land to speak.

III

We work through the body

Ancient wisdom was never stored in books. It was carried in the body — in how people walked, ate, slept, breathed, grieved, celebrated. Our work is embodied: somatic movement, dance, breathwork, nervous system regulation — not as techniques layered onto the journey, but as languages the body already knows. With the understanding that genuine safety comes first, and depth follows.

IV

We walk together

We keep our groups small — 10 to 15 people — because real connection does not happen in crowds. It happens in the quiet that develops between people who have shared difficulty and beauty in equal measure. We do not manage group dynamics; we trust them. Something generous emerges between people who have been slowed down together, held in a container of care, and moved through terrain larger than their concerns.

The Land

The land is not a backdrop

Every place carries its own intelligence — a way of knowing that existed long before language.

The desert teaches patience. The mountain teaches surrender. The olive grove reminds us that what grows slowly tends to give more.

We come to embody what is here, as it is — through the body, through attention, through being in relationship with the land.

In the Andean tradition, this is called munay — a quality of love that moves without force, without needing to arrive anywhere.

And slowly, what was always here begins to be felt again.

How this began

Merve Purde

Founder · Guide · Facilitator

Hello, I'm Merve — walking the path of love. I walk with a practice, a few trusted collaborators, and a genuine belief that the land still has something to offer us, if we arrive slowly enough to receive it.

Wisdom Walk began as a personal reckoning. After years of working in the world of conscious travel and somatic facilitation, I found myself asking: what would it look like to create the kind of journey I had always been searching for? One that moved slowly. That trusted silence. That worked with the body as well as the mind.

I have walked the sacred paths of Peru, sat in ceremony in India, harvested olives in the Aegean, and listened to the Himalayan wind. I have led groups through all of these landscapes — and what I have learned, again and again, is that the most important thing I can offer is not information. It is presence. The willingness to slow down, to be genuinely with people, and to trust that the land will do what words cannot.

Each journey I lead is small — deliberately so. I want to know the people I walk with. I want to remember their names, their questions, the moment something shifted for them on the trail. That intimacy is not incidental. It is the whole point.

We keep groups small — between 8 and 15 people. Not as a policy, but because we have learned that real connection needs a certain scale to form. Large groups create audiences. Small ones create community.
